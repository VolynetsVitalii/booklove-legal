What a reading tracker is actually for

A reading tracker is a record of three things: what you have read, what you are reading now, and what you mean to read next. That is the whole idea. Everything else - goals, statistics, timers - exists to make the record worth keeping.

Most people abandon a paper reading journal within a couple of months, and the reason is almost always the same: updating it costs more effort than the record gives back. So the useful question is not "which app has the most features", it is "which app makes the record cost almost nothing to keep".

Below are six ways of keeping a reading log, roughly from least to most effort, and what each one is actually good for.

One · the shelf

Four shelves and nothing to decide

Want to Read, Currently Reading, Read, Did Not Finish. Four is enough to answer every question you actually ask, and few enough that you never spend time organising the organiser.

The "Did Not Finish" shelf matters more than it looks. Without it, an abandoned book either sits in "Currently Reading" forever, quietly reproaching you, or gets deleted - and then you buy it again in two years having forgotten why you stopped.

Two · the scanner

Adding a book should take four seconds

Point the camera at the barcode on the back cover. The title, author, cover, page count and description arrive on their own.

This is the single thing that decides whether a reading log survives. Typing a title, an author and a page count by hand takes about a minute per book; do that thirty times and the habit dies. Scanning a whole shelf without stopping between books turns an afternoon of data entry into a few minutes.

Three · the goal

A number you chose, on a screen you already look at

A yearly reading goal works because it turns an intention into a number, and a Home Screen widget works because it puts the number where you already look forty times a day.

Set the goal honestly - a target you miss by half stops meaning anything by March. If a yearly number feels too far away, a daily page target gives the same nudge on a scale you can act on tonight.

Four · the timer and the streak

Minutes read, days in a row

Start the timer when you open the book, stop it when you put it down. The session is logged with the pages you reached, and the streak counts the days you read at all.

A streak is a blunt instrument, and that is the point: it does not care how much you read, only that you did. Ten minutes counts. That is exactly the pressure a habit needs and the pressure a page target cannot apply.

Five · the statistics

The part that makes the record worth keeping

Books finished, pages turned, your best year, your genres, your longest streak - and a Yearly Chronicle listing everything you finished, month by month.

Statistics are the payoff for the logging. Nobody keeps a record for the sake of the record; they keep it because in December it turns into a story about the year. Monthly Wrapped cards are that story in a form you can send to a friend.

Six · quotes, series and notes

The things you want to find again

Save a quote by photographing the page. Track a series so you know which volume is next and which one you skipped. Keep private notes and personal tags on any book.

This is the layer that turns a list into a library. A year later the thing you want is rarely "which books did I read" - it is "what was that line about the lighthouse, and which book was it in".

Three questions worth asking about any reading tracker

How do books get in?

If adding a book takes a minute of typing, the log will not survive contact with a busy month. Barcode scanning and catalogue search are not conveniences here - they are the difference between a habit and an abandoned app.

How do books get out?

Ask before you start, not after three years. Can you export everything, in a format something else can read, without paying? If the answer is no, you are not keeping a record - you are renting one.

Who else can see it?

A reading history is a surprisingly personal document: illness, grief, faith, sexuality and everything you were curious about at two in the morning are all visible in it. It is reasonable to want that private by default rather than private by configuration.

BooksLove answers those three like this: books come in by camera or search, everything goes out to CSV or JSON free at any time, and there is no account and nowhere to publish - your shelves are private from the first launch. The Privacy Policy names every outside service the app talks to.
